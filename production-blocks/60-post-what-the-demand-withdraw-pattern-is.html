What the demand-withdraw pattern is, and why it feeds itself

One partner raises a problem and pushes for change. The other goes quiet, changes the subject or leaves the room. The pushing gets harder, and so does the retreat. Relationship researchers have studied this loop for decades, and the most useful finding is about who ends up in which role.

What the pattern is

The demand-withdraw pattern is a loop in couple conflict: one partner raises a problem, criticises and asks for change, and the other avoids the discussion. Each response tends to provoke more of the other. Research suggests it is linked to lower satisfaction, and that the role a person takes depends more on who wants the change than on who they are.

Key takeaways
  • One partner demands change; the other avoids the discussion.
  • A meta-analysis of 74 studies linked the pattern to worse relationship and communication outcomes.
  • The harm was about equal whichever partner demanded.
  • Whoever wants the change tends to demand, in men and women, and in same-sex couples.
  • Researchers believe the pattern is self-perpetuating: withdrawal invites more demands, which invite more withdrawal.
  • Its effects are not uniform, and the links are associations, not proven causes.

This essay sets out what the research found and where it is still uncertain. It is meant to help people recognise the pattern, not to assign blame to the partner in either role.

Definition
Demand and withdraw

As Holley, Sturm and Levenson summarise the classic description, the pattern is characterised by one partner trying to discuss problems, criticising or blaming their partner, and requesting or demanding change, while the other partner tries to avoid discussion of the problem.

Where the idea came from

The pattern is most closely associated with Andrew Christensen, a clinical psychologist and Distinguished Research Professor in the UCLA Department of Psychology, whose research interests include conflict in couples and couples therapy.

In a 1990 study, Christensen and Heavey observed 31 couples in two conflict discussions, each about a change one of the partners wanted. The abstract reports that wife-demand and husband-withdraw interaction was more likely than the reverse overall, but only when discussing a change the wife wanted. Both husband and wife were more likely to be demanding when discussing a change they wanted.

The same study found a gender difference, but not the one usually assumed: men were overall more withdrawn than women, but women were not overall more demanding than men.

How researchers measure it

There are two main ways to study the pattern. One is to watch it happen: couples are asked to discuss a real disagreement and their behaviour is rated. The 1993 study described below, for example, had couples take part in two videotaped discussions.

The other is to ask. The Communication Patterns Questionnaire asks each partner how they and their partner typically behave when a problem comes up. A 2016 paper by Crenshaw, Christensen and colleagues reports that all four scales of a revised version of the questionnaire distinguished distressed from non-distressed couples, which is part of why it has been so widely used.

Both methods have limits. Observed discussions are short and staged; questionnaires depend on how people see their own relationship. The findings below draw on both, which is one reason to take the overall pattern seriously.

Who demands depends on the issue

The most useful finding in this research is that the roles follow the issue. A 1993 replication by Heavey, Layne and Christensen, with 29 married couples, found that when discussing the wives’ issues, wives were more demanding and husbands more withdrawing.

A later study widened the test. In a 2010 study of 63 heterosexual, gay and lesbian couples, Holley, Sturm and Levenson found that the demand-withdraw pattern was seen regardless of type of couple, and concluded that men and women are not inherently different in their tendencies to demand and withdraw. The more one person wants change, they write, the more likely that person is to demand while the partner withdraws, regardless of the sex of the partners.

Why, then, do women demand more often on average? The same authors suggest the pattern may result from social conventions that afford men greater power and women less power: on this reading, the partner who is more satisfied with how things are has less reason to push for change.

Watch out
Not a fixed trait

The popular version, that women nag and men withdraw, is not what the research shows. On average women are more often in the demanding role, but the role follows the issue and who wants the change, and the same pattern appears in couples of the same sex.

What the pattern is linked to

The largest summary of the evidence is a 2014 meta-analysis by Schrodt, Witt and Shimkowski, which reviewed 74 studies with 14,255 participants. It found a moderate, meaningful relationship between demand-withdraw and overall outcomes, a correlation of .360. The links were stronger for relational outcomes (.423) and communication outcomes (.418) than for demographic (.239) and well-being outcomes (.249).

Two details stand out. The direction did not matter much: wife-demand and husband-withdraw had a correlation of .380 with outcomes, and husband-demand and wife-withdraw .392. And the links were stronger in distressed or clinical samples (.413) than in others (.345).

The authors are careful about what this means. They describe the effects as reciprocal associations rather than causal links: a struggling relationship may produce the pattern as much as the pattern produces the struggle. Schrodt, in a press release about the study, put the practical point simply: “It’s also a very hard pattern to break.”

Why it feeds itself

The pattern is usually described as a loop. Ross, Karney, Nguyen and Bradbury, in a 2019 paper, put it this way: the demand-withdraw pattern is believed to be self-perpetuating, with disengagement inviting increasingly insistent requests for change, thus evoking even greater avoidance and defensiveness from the partner. Note the word believed: it is the field’s understanding of the mechanism, not a measured fact.

Part of what keeps the loop going is how each partner sees it. In the same press release, Schrodt explains that partners get locked in this pattern, largely because they each see the other as the cause. The person demanding sees withdrawal as the problem; the person withdrawing sees the demands as the problem. Both are describing the same loop from different points in it.

A related idea comes from John Gottman’s work. The Gottman Institute describes stonewalling as what happens when the listener shuts down because they feel overwhelmed or physiologically flooded, and recommends that a break from the argument should last at least twenty minutes, since it will take that much time for your bodies to physiologically calm down. Withdrawal, on this view, is sometimes less a tactic than a body overloaded. The institute also says that men are consistently more likely to stonewall than women, and that when women stonewall, it is quite predictive of divorce; these are its statements, not figures from a study linked here.

What it predicts

Studies that followed couples over time suggest the pattern matters for the future of a relationship, though the evidence is mixed in an important way. In the 1993 study, wife-demand and husband-withdraw interaction predicted a decline in wives’ satisfaction a year later, while husband-demand and wife-withdraw interaction predicted an increase in wives’ satisfaction.

Gottman and Levenson, in a 2000 study following couples for 14 years, built a measure of the wife-demand and husband-withdraw pattern from criticism and stonewalling, and found it predicted both earlier divorcing and later divorcing, with correlations of .32 and .27. A 2016 paper by Crenshaw, Christensen and colleagues summarises the wider literature: higher levels of demand-withdraw behaviour are associated with greater relationship distress and with a greater likelihood of divorce.

The pattern is also not always harmful. The 2019 study replicated the harmful effects of the pattern on wives’ satisfaction among relatively affluent couples, while documenting benefits of it among relatively disadvantaged couples. Its authors suggest that men’s withdrawal appears to be maladaptive when couples have resources and capacities, but may be adaptive when those resources and capacities are lacking. One way to read that, which is this essay’s gloss: when a problem cannot be solved, stepping back from it may protect the relationship.

Across cultures

The pattern is not only an American one. A 2006 study by Christensen and colleagues of 363 participants in Brazil, Italy, Taiwan and the United States found that woman-demand and man-withdraw communication was significantly more likely than the reverse, and that differences between partners in desire for closeness versus independence were associated with more demand-withdraw communication.

Across all four countries, the same study found, constructive communication was positively associated with relationship satisfaction, whereas demand-withdraw communication was negatively associated with it. The pattern, and its link to satisfaction, travelled across very different cultures.

The same study adds a clue about why the roles fall as they do. The abstract notes that some evidence suggested women wanted greater closeness versus independence in their relationships than did men. Combined with the finding that differences in that desire predicted more of the pattern, it points to a simple mechanism: the partner who wants more connection, or more change, pushes; the partner who is content with less steps back.

What helps

Because both partners are inside the loop, approaches that help them see it from outside seem promising. Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy, developed by Christensen and the late Neil S. Jacobson, aims, according to its UCLA site, to integrate the twin goals of acceptance and change, partly by helping couples identify the patterns that get them stuck.

It has been tested in a large trial. In a 2004 study of 134 seriously and chronically distressed married couples, 71% of couples receiving this therapy and 59% receiving a traditional behavioural couple therapy were reliably improved or recovered. At five years, the APA Monitor reported, about half the couples in each group still showed clinically significant improvement, and the difference between the two therapies had disappeared. None of the sources linked here says the therapy targets the demand-withdraw pattern specifically.

Five years on, the picture was mixed but encouraging. A UCLA release on the follow-up reported that about half the couples were significantly improved, about a quarter were separated or divorced, and about a quarter were unchanged. A 2011 analysis of the same trial records that 98 of the 134 couples, 73.1%, were still married at five years. For couples who began seriously distressed, that is a meaningful result, though not a cure.

Note
Not a substitute for help

This essay describes research. Couples who feel stuck in this loop, or in any pattern that is causing distress, may benefit from working with a qualified couples therapist. Withdrawal that is about fear or safety is a different matter from the pattern described here.

What this does and does not say

Read carefully, the research supports a clear and useful picture. The demand-withdraw loop is common, it is linked to worse outcomes in either direction, and the roles in it depend largely on who wants the change. That makes it a pattern between two people, not a flaw in one of them. The causal story is less certain than the loop makes it feel, and the pattern is not harmful in every circumstance.

In practice, the research suggests naming the loop together rather than blaming the other person for their part in it, and noticing which role each person tends to take on which issue. The person withdrawing may need a break to calm down; the person demanding may need to know the issue will actually be returned to.

And what rumination is looks at the inward version of going round in circles.

The short version

The demand-withdraw pattern is a common way couples get stuck in conflict: one partner tries to discuss a problem, criticises and asks for change, and the other avoids the discussion. A meta-analysis of 74 studies found it moderately associated with worse relationship, communication and well-being outcomes, in both directions, and described the links as reciprocal rather than causal. Women are more often in the demanding role on average, but research suggests the role follows the issue: whoever wants the change tends to demand, and the same pattern appears in gay and lesbian couples. The pattern is thought to feed itself, with withdrawal inviting more insistent demands and those inviting more withdrawal. Its effects are not uniform, and therapy that helps couples see the pattern has shown benefits.

Author's note

The 2014 meta-analysis is described from its abstract and press release. Correlations describe associations across studies, not effects on any one couple.
