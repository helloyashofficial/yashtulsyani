What physicists mean by extra dimensions, and how they look for them

Some of the most ambitious theories in physics need more dimensions of space than the three we know. Where would they be, and why can’t we see them? Physicists have answers, and they have built experiments to test them. So far, every test has come back empty.

What extra dimensions are

In physics, extra dimensions are additional directions of space beyond the three we move in, proposed in theories that try to unify the forces of nature. If they exist, they would be curled up very small, or otherwise hidden from everyday experience. Experiments have searched for them in particle collisions and in precise measurements of gravity, and have found nothing so far.

Key takeaways
  • The idea began in 1921, when Kaluza used a fifth dimension to combine gravity and electromagnetism.
  • Klein suggested the extra dimension could be curled up too small to notice.
  • String theory requires extra dimensions.
  • A 1998 proposal suggested extra dimensions might be large enough to change gravity below a millimetre.
  • The Large Hadron Collider and tabletop gravity tests have found no sign of them.
  • They have not been ruled out, only limited in size.

This essay is in the literal register, like what it means to call time the fourth dimension. The extra dimensions here are directions of space, not time, and not a metaphor; any borrowing for human life is kept to one labelled paragraph at the end.

Definition
Compactified dimension

A dimension of space that is curled up so small that it cannot be noticed. In the words of a historical paper on Oskar Klein, Klein suggested that the extra dimension could be compactified, and would therefore remain unobservable when it is small enough.

A fifth dimension to unify the forces

The idea goes back to Theodor Kaluza, a German mathematician. According to the MacTutor history of mathematics archive, Kaluza wrote to Einstein in April 1919 with a way of unifying gravity and electromagnetism by adding a fifth dimension; the work was published in 1921, with Einstein communicating the paper on 8 December of that year, after more than two years’ delay. Kaluza described the theory as possessing “virtually unsurpassed formal unity”.

The obvious problem was that nobody sees a fifth dimension. The Swedish physicist Oskar Klein proposed an answer. As Finn Ravndal of the University of Oslo puts it in a historical paper, the idea had previously been explored by Kaluza, but Klein made it more acceptable by suggesting that the extra dimension could be compactified and therefore remain unobservable when it is small enough. Klein’s papers appeared in 1926. The combined idea is now called Kaluza-Klein theory.

A common way to picture a compact dimension comes from a garden hose. As a Physics Today review of a book on the subject puts it, just as a garden hose appears one-dimensional from a distance large compared to the diameter of the hose, a dimension curled up small would be invisible on ordinary scales. Up close, the hose has a round direction that cannot be seen from far away.

String theory needs them

The idea of hidden dimensions returned with string theory. The physicist Sean Carroll put it plainly in Symmetry magazine: “String theory requires the existence of extra dimensions.”

How many depends on the version of the theory. In a Scientific American answer, the physicist Moshe Rozali explains that supersymmetry, a proposed symmetry that string theory relies on, limits the number of extra dimensions to six or seven, which with the four familiar dimensions of spacetime gives ten or eleven in total; M-theory is a proposal to further unify the different versions. He also notes that supersymmetry is still theoretical.

Watch out
Not proven

String theory is not established physics. It requires extra dimensions, but neither the theory nor the dimensions have been confirmed by experiment. Statements that string theory proves there are ten or eleven dimensions get the order wrong.

Large extra dimensions and the weakness of gravity

For most of the twentieth century, extra dimensions were assumed to be unimaginably small. In 1998 three physicists proposed something bolder. In a paper titled The Hierarchy Problem and New Dimensions at a Millimeter, Arkani-Hamed, Dimopoulos and Dvali suggested new compact spatial dimensions large compared to the weak scale, and noted that for the case of two new dimensions, planned sub-millimetre measurements of gravity may observe the transition.

The motivation is a puzzle called the hierarchy problem: why gravity is so much weaker than the other forces. The CERN explainer on extra dimensions sets out one answer: we don’t feel the full effect of gravity because part of it spreads to extra dimensions. On this picture, gravity is diluted by spreading where the other forces do not.

A year later Randall and Sundrum proposed a different mechanism. Their 1999 paper, A Large Mass Hierarchy from a Small Extra Dimension, described a new higher-dimensional mechanism for solving the hierarchy problem that relies on the existence of only a single additional dimension, with the large difference in scales arising from the geometry of spacetime itself.

Why gravity is the place to look

Both modern proposals share a feature: extra dimensions show up mainly through gravity. That is partly because of what they were invented to explain. The 1998 paper took, in its abstract’s words, the weak scale as the only fundamental short distance scale in nature, with the gravitational and gauge interactions becoming united at that scale. In plain terms, gravity would be as strong as the other forces at very short distances, and would only look weak at the distances we can measure.

The 1999 warped model predicts its own signature. Its abstract notes that there are fundamental spin-2 excitations with mass of weak scale order: heavy cousins of the graviton, the hypothetical particle that carries gravity, which a collider might produce.

String theory points the same way. The authors of the 2020 gravity test, in their paper, explain that string theory’s unification of gravity with the other forces inherently involves extra gravitational space dimensions. Testing gravity at the shortest distances possible is one of the few ways to probe that directly.

The CERN explainer gives the general principle for why such dimensions could be hidden at all: if one dimension were to contract to a size smaller than an atom, it would be hidden from our view. Everyday experience, and most experiments, simply never look closely enough.

Looking for them at the LHC

If extra dimensions are large enough, the Large Hadron Collider at CERN might reveal them. The CERN explainer describes several possible signs. Particles called gravitons, if produced, would rapidly disappear into extra dimensions, leaving an empty zone that we notice as an imbalance in momentum and energy. There might be heavier versions of particles, called Kaluza-Klein states. And if the scale of gravity were low enough, microscopic black holes might form, and would disintegrate rapidly.

None of these has been seen. Symmetry magazine had noted in its 2006 survey that CERN’s LHC would continue the search once it started running. A 2016 ATLAS search for microscopic black holes, in models with two to six extra dimensions, reported: “No excess of events beyond Standard Model predictions is observed.” A CMS search for events with a single photon and missing energy excluded values of the fundamental Planck scale up to 2.31 to 2.49 TeV, depending on the number of extra dimensions. An ATLAS search published in 2021, using the full dataset of the collider’s second run, found overall agreement between the data and the Standard Model predictions.

The most recent search for microscopic black holes, a CMS paper revised in August 2026 using data from 2016 to 2018, reports that no excesses above the standard model background predictions are observed, and in the context of models with large extra dimensions excludes semiclassical black holes and string balls with masses below 8.4 to 11.4 TeV. As Symmetry magazine put it in 2013, scientists are watching, but so far, they haven’t found signs of microscopic black holes.

Testing gravity on a tabletop

The other way to look is to measure gravity itself at very short distances. Newton’s law says gravity weakens with the square of the distance. As the University of Washington explains, this law, called an inverse-square law, is based purely on geometry, but if the universe has more than three spatial dimensions, the inverse-square law would break.

The Eöt-Wash group at the University of Washington has tested it directly. In a 2020 paper, Lee, Adelberger, Cook, Fleischer and Heckel reported measurements at separations between 52 micrometres and 3 millimetres. Their conclusion: “Newtonian gravity gave an excellent fit to our data.”

The same measurement constrains other proposed particles too. The paper reports that any gravitational-strength force of the Yukawa type, a force that switches off beyond a certain range, must have a range less than 38.6 micrometres, and that two hypothetical particles from extra-dimension models, the dilaton and the radion, are pushed to masses above 5.1 meV and above a unification scale of 7.1 TeV respectively. Every one of these limits came from finding exactly what Newton predicted.

That sets a limit. In the full paper, the authors conclude that the largest extra dimension must have a toroidal radius less than 30 µm, if extra dimensions are of the kind the 1998 proposal described. The millimetre-sized dimensions that proposal imagined are ruled out; much smaller ones are not.

What has and has not been ruled out

The searches do not prove that extra dimensions do not exist. They rule out particular sizes and energy scales in particular models. A curled-up dimension much smaller than the experiments can probe, as in the original Kaluza-Klein idea or in many versions of string theory, would leave no trace in either kind of test.

The honest summary has not changed much since Symmetry magazine described the field’s embarrassing dilemma in 2006: not a single shred of experimental evidence has been found to support their existence. Two decades and a great deal of data later, the dilemma stands. The window for large extra dimensions has narrowed; the idea itself remains open.

What this does and does not say

Read literally, extra dimensions are a serious, testable idea in physics with no evidence in their favour so far. They are directions of space, curled up or otherwise hidden, proposed to make the laws of nature simpler or to explain why gravity is weak. They are not time, they are not other worlds or planes of consciousness, and they are not proven.

Here is the one borrowing in this essay, labelled as such. As a metaphor only, the garden hose is a useful reminder that something can look simpler from a distance than it is up close. In physics that is a claim about geometry that experiments are testing; in a life it is only a resemblance, and nothing here suggests the physics governs it.

The short version

Extra dimensions in physics are additional directions of space, beyond the familiar three, proposed to unify the forces. Theodor Kaluza showed in 1921 that a fifth dimension could combine gravity and electromagnetism, and Oskar Klein suggested such a dimension could be curled up too small to see. String theory requires extra dimensions. In 1998 a proposal suggested they might be as large as a millimetre, with gravity spreading into them, which would explain why gravity is so weak, and in 1999 another proposal used a single warped extra dimension. Experiments have looked: the Large Hadron Collider has found no excess of events that extra dimensions would produce, including no microscopic black holes, and precision measurements show Newton’s law of gravity holding down to 52 micrometres, limiting the largest extra dimension to less than 30 micrometres. There is no experimental evidence for extra dimensions, and they have not been ruled out.

Frequently asked

What are extra dimensions in physics?
Additional directions of space, beyond the familiar three, proposed in theories that try to unify the forces of nature. If they exist, they would be curled up very small or otherwise hidden from everyday experience.
Is there any evidence for extra dimensions?
No. Particle collider searches and precise measurements of gravity have found no sign of them. They have been limited in size, not ruled out.
How many dimensions does string theory need?
Ten or eleven in total, depending on the version, counting the four of spacetime, according to a Scientific American explainer by the physicist Moshe Rozali. Neither string theory nor its extra dimensions has been confirmed by experiment.
Could the Large Hadron Collider make black holes?
Some extra-dimension models predicted microscopic black holes at the LHC, which would disintegrate rapidly. Searches have found none, and the most recent CMS search excludes them below about 8.4 to 11.4 TeV in large extra dimension models.
Is time one of the extra dimensions?
No. Time is the fourth dimension of spacetime in relativity. The extra dimensions proposed in physics are additional dimensions of space, beyond the three we know.
