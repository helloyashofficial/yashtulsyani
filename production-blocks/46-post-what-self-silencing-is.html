What self-silencing is, and what keeping the peace can cost

Not saying the thing, so that the evening stays calm, can look like kindness. Psychologists have a name and a scale for it, and the research on what it does over time is clearer than the advice that circulates about it.

What self-silencing is

Self-silencing is holding back your own thoughts, feelings and needs in a close relationship to avoid conflict or the risk of losing the other person. The research treats it as a pattern of beliefs about how to keep a relationship, measured by a questionnaire, and it has been linked repeatedly to depression.

Key takeaways
  • The idea comes from the psychologist Dana Crowley Jack, who built it from interviews with depressed women.
  • It is measured by a 31-item questionnaire with four parts, from judging yourself by others’ standards to keeping a public self apart from a private one.
  • The research links it to depressive symptoms; most of that evidence is correlational, and some of it follows people over time.
  • Men often score as high as women or higher. What the silence means to them can differ.
  • Hiding what you feel when you give something up for a partner tracks with lower satisfaction later, though not for everyone.

This essay sets out where the idea came from, what the questionnaire measures, what the evidence does and does not show, and where holding back is ordinary judgement rather than a problem.

Definition
Self-silencing

Not saying, doing or showing things you think would contradict a partner’s wishes, so as to protect the relationship. In Dana Crowley Jack’s account, it rests on beliefs about how intimacy has to be kept, not on a single decision to stay quiet.

Where the idea came from

The concept comes from Dana Crowley Jack, who developed it from listening to depressed women talk about their relationships, set out in her 1991 book Silencing the Self: Women and Depression. In an extract written with her co-author Ali, Jack describes women who would silence or suppress certain thoughts, feelings and actions that they thought would contradict their partner’s wishes, and argues that these beliefs create vulnerability to depression by directing women to defer to the needs of others.

To study the idea, Jack and Dill published the Silencing the Self Scale in 1992. As the scale’s record sets out, it has 31 statements, each rated on a five-point scale from disagree to agree, giving a total between 31 and 155. Jack and Ali note that the statements were taken from depressed women’s own accounts and worded so that they apply equally to men.

One item, as quoted in a published study that used the scale, gives the flavour: “I don’t speak my feelings in an intimate relationship when I know they will cause disagreement.” Most people will recognise that sentence as true of themselves sometimes. The scale is about how often, and how much it organises the relationship.

What the research links it to

The central finding is an association with depression. Across many studies, people who score higher on self-silencing tend to report more depressive symptoms. That is a correlation, and on its own it cannot say which comes first: silence could feed low mood, low mood could make people quieter, or both could share a cause.

Some studies follow people over time, which helps. In a study of 209 adolescent dating couples, published in 2011, self-silencing predicted increases in depressive symptoms from the first measurement to the second. That points in one direction for that group. It is one study, in young couples, and it does not rule out the reverse.

Much of the rest of the evidence is measured at a single point. A 2026 systematic review of studies in women, posted as a preprint that has not yet been peer reviewed, describes the evidence as converging on a consistent positive relationship, and notes that most studies are cross-sectional, which limits causal inference.

Note
What this essay leaves out

A 2021 meta-analysis pooled studies of self-silencing and depression. Its published abstract could not be opened for this essay, and its figures circulate mainly on secondary sites, so none of them are repeated here.

Not only women

Because the idea began with women’s accounts, it is often described as a women’s pattern. The scores do not bear that out. In a 2010 study of informal cancer carers by Ussher and Perz, with 329 women and 155 men, the men reported greater self-silencing than the women. The women reported more depression and anxiety, and both were correlated with self-silencing.

Other studies agree on the scores and add detail. In a study of 211 adolescents and their partners, boys reported more self-silencing than girls. In a 2013 study of 247 college students, women scored higher on judging themselves by outside standards, men scored higher on care as self-sacrifice, and that self-sacrifice subscale was negatively correlated with depression in men and unrelated to it in women.

What may differ is the meaning. In interviews in the Ussher and Perz study, women tended to describe silencing themselves as something they had to do to cope, while men tended to describe it as an ordinary part of being a man, and saw voicing their own needs as weakness. The same score can sit on different reasons.

What silence does inside a relationship

The closest evidence on couples comes from research on hiding emotions. In a study by Impett and colleagues, couples took part in a laboratory session, kept a diary for fourteen days and were followed up three months later. People who suppressed their emotions when they made a sacrifice for their partner reported decreases in relationship satisfaction, and more thoughts about breaking up three months later. Feeling less authentic explained much of that cost.

A later study of 80 couples looked at the other side. When people believed their partner was hiding feelings while making a sacrifice, they saw that partner as less authentic, and that went with poorer relationship quality three months on.

Self-silencing as Jack defined it has also been followed over years. In a study that followed 144 adolescents from 16 to 23, self-silencing at 16 predicted more concealment in relationships seven years later, and changes in self-silencing were related to later concealment, partner support and relationship certainty. And in a 2001 doctoral study of 85 couples, silencing was associated with an increase in women’s depressive symptoms, though not men’s.

The last row is often quoted as if it were about couples. It is not. In the 2003 study by Butler and colleagues, the pairs were strangers. Suppression disrupted communication, reduced rapport and magnified blood pressure responses in the suppressor’s partner in the conversation. It is a striking finding about what hiding emotion does to an exchange, but it was measured between people who had just met. A broader 2003 study by Gross and John found that habitually suppressing emotion was associated with worse interpersonal functioning and lower well-being.

When holding back is not a mistake

None of this means every unsaid thought is a wound. Ordinary tact, choosing a better moment and letting small things go are part of living with anyone. The research is about a pattern, not a single silence.

There is also a real exception. In a study by Le and Impett, people who were highly focused on their partner and the relationship, which the authors call high interdependence, experienced boosts in well-being and relationship quality when they suppressed negative emotions during a sacrifice. The authors describe this as a condition under which suppression may be beneficial.

The distinction that matters, then, is between choosing not to raise something and routinely making yourself smaller to keep the peace. The first is judgement. The second is what the studies keep finding costs something.

Silence and reassurance, two ends of one fear

Self-silencing has an opposite that this site has already written about. The essay on reassurance seeking describes asking again and again for proof that a relationship is safe. Self-silencing tries to keep it safe by asking for nothing at all. One says too much and the other too little, and both are trying to manage the other person’s reaction before it happens.

That is where an older idea is useful. In the language of what is up to us, what you say and how you say it are yours; how the other person responds is not. Silencing yourself to control that response puts effort on the side of the line where it cannot work, and gives up the part that was yours.

The research on attachment points the same way. Fear of rejection is one of the two dimensions described in what attachment styles actually measure, and self-silencing is one of the things that fear can lead people to do. A score does not decide the behaviour; the behaviour is the part that can change.

What to try instead

No trial has tested a cure for self-silencing, so what follows is drawn from the findings above rather than proven by them. The most consistent thread is authenticity: in the Impett studies, feeling less authentic explained much of the cost of hiding emotion.

Nobody has to shrink, one of the books on this site, is about the person being made small in a relationship, as its own page puts it, and turns the same mirror on the person doing it. This essay is the research underneath one half of that: what it costs, over time, to keep making yourself smaller so that nothing has to be said.

The short version

Self-silencing means holding back your own thoughts, feelings and needs to avoid conflict and keep a relationship. Frequently asked

What is self-silencing?
Holding back your own thoughts, feelings and needs in a close relationship to avoid conflict or the risk of losing the other person. The idea comes from the psychologist Dana Crowley Jack and is measured with the 31-item Silencing the Self Scale.
Is self-silencing linked to depression?
Yes, consistently, though mostly through correlations measured at one time. One study of 209 adolescent dating couples found that self-silencing predicted later increases in depressive symptoms, which suggests a direction for that group but does not rule out the reverse.
Do men self-silence too?
Yes. In several studies men scored as high as women or higher, including a study of 155 men and 329 women caring for someone with cancer. Interviews in that study suggested the silence can mean something different to men than to women.
Is it always bad to hide your feelings from a partner?
No. Ordinary tact and choosing your moment are part of any relationship, and one study found that people highly focused on their partner benefited from suppressing negative emotions during a sacrifice. The costs show up when hiding becomes a habit, especially around giving things up for a partner.
What is the Silencing the Self Scale?
A 31-item questionnaire published by Jack and Dill in 1992. It has four parts: judging yourself by outside standards, care as self-sacrifice, silencing the self, and a divided self.
