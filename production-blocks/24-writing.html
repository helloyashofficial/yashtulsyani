    What a tesseract is, and how one Victorian tried to teach people to see it

    A tesseract is a cube taken one dimension further: a four-dimensional figure with 16 corners and 8 cubes for sides. The word was coined by Charles Howard Hinton, a Victorian mathematician who believed that, with enough practice with coloured cubes, people could learn to picture it. What you see in the famous rotating images is not the tesseract itself, but its shadow.

THE FIVE SUBJECTS

Five subjects, one preoccupation

They are separated below because a reader deserves to know which register a piece is written in, particularly where the physics and the metaphor sit close together. They are not really five things.

Relationships
Insecurity, validation, neglect, and the mechanics by which a fear about a person produces the behaviour that confirms it.
Philosophy
Mostly questions about determinism, participation and what it means to be a cause of something rather than a witness to it.
Psychology
What the research actually supports, held at arm's length from the version of it that circulates as advice.
Physics and time
The literal register. Measurement, and the parts of quantum mechanics that are genuinely strange rather than rhetorically convenient.
Fourth dimension
Time as a dimension: what spacetime means in physics, the philosophical questions it leaves open, such as whether the past still exists, and the older fourth dimension it is often confused with. Physics and philosophy are labelled as each.
